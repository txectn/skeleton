How to repot a fiddle-leaf fig without the drama

The right pot, the right hour of the day, and one small trick that keeps the leaves from sulking for weeks afterwards.

Figs hate two things: being moved, and being moved badly. Pick a pot just five centimetres wider than the old one — anything bigger and the wet soil sits around roots that can’t drink it yet, which is exactly where rot begins.

Water the night before, do the move before noon, and keep the plant out of direct sun for three days. It will drop a leaf or two in protest. That’s theatre, not tragedy — new growth usually resumes within a month.

Run the same loop every Sunday: finger into the soil up to the second knuckle, check the newest leaves for pests underneath, rotate every pot a quarter turn, and dust anything glossy with a damp cloth. Ten minutes, and problems get caught while they’re still small.

Fenestrations are earned, not given. A monstera only splits its leaves when it has enough light to afford the holes — in the wild, that means climbing toward the canopy. Give yours the brightest indirect light you can, be patient with fertiliser, and let the top few centimetres of soil dry out between waterings. Usually the next leaf is the one that finally splits.

The studio

Grown by hand, packed with heart

Fernway started in 2016 with a single shelf of cuttings in a Gowanus storefront and a stubborn belief that a good plant changes a room more than any piece of furniture.

Nine years later we grow forty-odd species across two greenhouses, still propagate most of what we sell, and still write the care card for every plant by hand. Nothing ships looking tired — if it isn’t thriving on our benches, it doesn’t leave.
